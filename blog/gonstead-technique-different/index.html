What Makes the Gonstead Technique Different?

Five ways of looking at your spine, one specific adjustment. Here is how the Gonstead approach works and why it starts with analysis instead of a routine.

November 11, 2026 · West Salem Chiropractic

Chiropractic visits can look very different from office to office. Some feel quick and routine. Others involve more looking, measuring and explaining before anyone is adjusted. The Gonstead Technique sits firmly in the second group. At West Salem Chiropractic, Dr. Daniel Eoriatti uses the Gonstead approach, and this article explains what that means for you as a patient.

A technique with Wisconsin roots

The Gonstead Technique is named after Dr. Clarence Gonstead, who grew up on a dairy farm in Primrose, Wisconsin, and completed his chiropractic studies at Palmer College of Chiropractic in 1923. Alongside his chiropractic studies, he pursued a career as an automotive engineer. That mechanical background shaped how he thought about the spine. He looked at it the way an engineer looks at a structure that carries weight: what is level, what is not, and where the load is going.

That way of thinking is still at the heart of the method. Rather than adjusting every area that feels tight, a Gonstead chiropractor tries to find the specific segment that is causing the problem and to correct that segment alone. If you want the longer story, our page on Dr. Gonstead covers his life and work.

Specificity is the point

The single idea that sets the Gonstead Technique apart is specificity. Dr. Gonstead taught that adjusting the wrong vertebral segment can create a new problem rather than solve the original one. So the goal is not to move as much of the spine as possible. The goal is to find the exact place that needs attention and to adjust it in the right direction with the right amount of force.

Finding that place takes more than one test. A single finding can be misleading on its own. That is why the Gonstead System uses five separate components of analysis, and why the doctor looks for them to agree before an adjustment is made.

The five components of analysis

1. Visualization

Analysis starts the moment you walk in. The doctor watches how you move, how you stand and how you sit down. Differences in the height of your ears, shoulders and hips, the way you hold your head, and the way you walk can all point toward the area worth examining more closely. It adds context to everything that follows.

2. Instrumentation

Gonstead chiropractors use a dual probe instrument, such as a Nervoscope or Delta-T, that glides along either side of the spine and compares skin temperature from left to right. An uneven reading can suggest inflammation at that level. Over time, the same readings help show whether an area is settling down or still needs attention.

3. Palpation

Palpation simply means examining with the hands, and the Gonstead method uses two kinds. Static palpation is done while you are still, feeling for swelling, tenderness, tone, texture and temperature. Motion palpation is done while the doctor gently moves each segment, feeling for joints that are stuck or not moving the way they should. Together, they help separate a stiff joint from an irritated one.

4. X-ray analysis

Where X-rays are appropriate, the Gonstead System favors full-spine films taken both from the front and from the side, covering the spine from the pelvis to the base of the skull. Seeing the whole spine at once shows the alignment and the disc spaces directly, gives an accurate count of the vertebrae, and can reveal issues beyond the spot that hurts. It also means the adjustment is aimed at something measured rather than assumed.

5. Case management

Your history and symptoms make up the fifth component. What hurts, when it started, what makes it better or worse, and how it affects your day all help narrow down where the problem is coming from. This is not applied like a recipe. It is one more line of evidence that has to fit with the other four.

Why the disc gets so much attention

Gonstead chiropractors are sometimes called "disc doctors." Dr. Gonstead placed strong emphasis on the intervertebral discs, the cushions between the vertebrae that act as both shock absorbers and pivots for movement. His view was that the spine works best when the discs are level, so that weight spreads evenly and each joint moves the way it was designed to.

When a vertebra shifts out of its normal position, the disc beneath it no longer sits level. In the Gonstead model, that uneven pressure can irritate the disc and the nerves nearby. This is why the analysis pays so much attention to the disc spaces on the X-rays, and why the direction of each adjustment matters as much as its location.

What the adjustment itself is like

Gonstead adjustments are done by hand. Your position depends on the area being adjusted and is chosen so the doctor can work on one segment without twisting the rest of your spine.

The adjustment is quick and specific. Most people describe a feeling of pressure and a brief movement, sometimes with an audible release. The doctor will tell you what is about to happen before it happens. If anything feels uncomfortable, say so, and the approach can be changed. Nothing about it should have you bracing yourself.

Because every patient is different, the force and positioning are matched to you. A plan for a young athlete does not look the same as a plan for a grandparent, even if both come in with low back pain.

Checking every visit, not just the first

Because the method depends on precision, the analysis does not stop after the first appointment. At each visit, the spine is checked again for subtle changes that may call for a different level or direction of adjustment. If an area has settled, it is left alone. That is part of what keeps Gonstead care specific over time rather than repeating the same routine week after week.

Is the Gonstead Technique right for you?

The only honest way to answer that is with an examination. Not every problem is mechanical, and some belong with a physician, a specialist or imaging first. Part of a good first visit is finding out which is which. If the findings point somewhere other than chiropractic care, you should hear that plainly.

If you are curious about what an evaluation involves, our page on your first visit walks through it step by step, and The Gonstead System page covers the five components in more detail. You can also read about chiropractic care at our office.

The short version

  • The Gonstead Technique looks for the specific segment causing a problem and adjusts only that segment.
  • Five components of analysis are used: visualization, instrumentation, palpation, X-ray analysis and case management.
  • Full-spine X-rays, where appropriate, show alignment and disc spaces directly.
  • Adjustments are done by hand, with positioning chosen to isolate one area at a time.
  • The spine is checked again at each visit so care stays specific as things change.
